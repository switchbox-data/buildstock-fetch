# Modeling EV demand on top of 2024 ResStock data

We propose a procedure for appending electric vehicle (EV) charging profiles to a sample of buildings from the 2024 ResStock release. Our goal is to quantify the impact of time-of-use (TOU) rates on EV charging behavior and estimate how much EV owners can save on their electric bills as a result. 

By integrating the EV charging load with the residential base electric load, we can analyze the impact of TOU rates on household load profiles. Down the line, we aim to understand if/how EV owners may be over or underpaying on their electric bills by comparing the costs they impose on the grid with the revenue generated by the utilities. This in turn can suggest how to design more fair and efficient rates.

Our procedure is outlined below.

## 1. Assigning EVs to ResStock buildings

First, given a sample of ResStock buildings generated from the 2024 release, we need to assign EVs to households. To do so, we use the EV_Ownership.tsv file (https://github.com/NatLabRockies/resstock/tree/develop/project_national/housing_characteristics), which was published as part of the 2025 ResStock release and provides EV ownership rates by federal poverty level (FPL), building type, tenure, and PUMA. As is done in the 2025 ResStock release, we also assume each household has at most one EV.

We directly apply these rates to ResStock buildings sampled from the 2024 release, but the sake of understanding assumptions used and how we might replicate this at higher EV adoption rates, we outline the procedure for how EV_Ownership.tsv was generated. These steps are explained in more detail in the NLR's EV Integration Report (https://www.osti.gov/biblio/2584243) and Technical Reference Guide (https://oedi-data-lake.s3.amazonaws.com/nrel-pds-building-stock/end-use-load-profiles-for-us-building-stock/2025/resstock_amy2018_release_1/ResStockTechnicalReferenceGuide_2025_1.pdf). 

1. The 2023 Experian dataset provides county-level vehicle registration data. By combining this with the 2016 American Community Survey (ACS) data that documents vehicles per household by county, they estimate the fraction of households with an EV by county. In terms of math, for each county, they compute EVs per household = (# registered EVs / total vehicle regstrations) * (vehicles per household).
2. The county-level EV ownership rates were then mapped to the PUMA level (using census-tract weighted averages and assuming rates are constant throughout each county) and joined with the building characteristic data from the 2019 5-year PUMS dataset.
3. Finally, using the U.S. EIA 2020 Residential Energy Consumption Survey (RECS) data, they have national-level EV ownership rates by FPL, building type, and tenure. These probabilities are scaled to match the PUMA-level EV ownership rates from step 2.

In terms of math, they have:
Experian and ACS mapped to PUMAs: P(EV | PUMA)
PUMS: P(FPL, building type, tenure | PUMA)
RECS: P(EV | FPL, building type, tenure)

To obtain P(EV | PUMA, FPL, building type, tenure), they use the law of total probability to get the relationship:
P (EV | PUMA) = \sum P(EV | PUMA, FPL, building type, tenure) * P (FPL, building type, tenure | PUMA).

They apply proportional scaling, setting P(EV | PUMA, FPL, building type, tenure) = k_{PUMA} * P(EV | FPL, building type, tenure). Combining this with the above equation, they solve for each k_{PUMA} and thereby obtain P(EV | PUMA, FPL, building type, tenure).

If we wanted to scale to future higher EV adoption rates (i.e., rates above levels reported in the 2023 Experian vehicle registration dataset), we would just need to plug in new county- or PUMA-level EV adoption rates to the above procedure.

In our code, we use Bernoulli sampling to apply the EV ownership rates to a given ResStock sample. This is in contrast to the quota-based sampling applied in the 2025 ResStock release, which as noted in the Technical Reference Guide (https://oedi-data-lake.s3.amazonaws.com/nrel-pds-building-stock/end-use-load-profiles-for-us-building-stock/2025/resstock_amy2018_release_1/ResStockTechnicalReferenceGuide_2025_1.pdf), can lead to underestimates when adoption rates are low (for example, if in a PUMA-FPL-building type-tenure segment, the expected number of EVs is 0.4, this gets rounded down to 0 and no EVs are sampled, and this compounds across several segments with low adoption rates). We confirmed this empirically in ResStock buildings sampled from the 2025 release for the state of Maryland, where we expected 165 EVs across 8,954 occupied buildings but only 127 were assigned. This suggests either applying quota-based sampling to expanded segments or switching to Bernoulli sampling.

Moreover, we note that the NLR assumes vacant units do not have EVs, so all of the above probabilities are occupied-conditional. In our code, we set vacant ResStock buildings to have zero EVs and only apply the calculated EV ownership rates to occupied segments. The NLR also assumes that households and dwelling units are synonymous when pulling from ACS and PUMS data. 

## 2. Assigning EV charging attributes

We plan to develop methods to assign these attributes with more granularity, but for now, we assume that all EVs:
- have a battery capacity of 90 kWh,
- are charged with a Level 2 charger (that is available whenever needed) with power 7.2 kW, and
- do all of their charging at home.

## 3. Matching NHTS vehicle profiles to EV owners

In this step, we use data from the 2022 National Household Travel Survey. Participants were asked to keep track of all trips over a 24 hour period from 4am one day to 3:59am the next day (see the User Guide: https://nhts.ornl.gov/media/2022/doc/2022%20NextGen%20NHTS%20User's%20Guide%20V201_PubUse.pdf). For each trip, they catalog start time, end time, and miles traveled. We currently assume that all trips start and end at home, though future work should also categorize start and end destinations and whether the vehicle is at home, work, school, etc. This data (or at least some of it) should also be logged with the rest of the NHTS dataset.

We first filter the NHTS data for the state in our chosen ResStock sample. Then, for each household with an EV, we match one NHTS weekday daily trip profile and one NHTS weekend daily trip profile based on income buckets, the number of household occupants, and the number of vehicles. When an exact match is not available across all three categories, we match based on just income and the number of occupants or just based on income.

We model trips with an hourly level of granularity, so we map the start and end times (in minutes) of each logged trip in the NHTS dataset as follows:
- the departure hour is the hour of the trip start time (e.g., 9:15am gets departure hour = 9am, 10:50am gets departure hour 10am)
- if the trip end time is exactly on the hour (e.g., 10:00am), then the arrival hour is exactly this time. Otherwise, the arrival hour is the the end time rounded up to the next hour (e.g., 9:30 gets arrival hour = 10am).

So, for example, a trip from 9:17am to 9:50am is logged as 9am departure from home and 10am arrival back home. As another example, a trip from 9:50am to 10:15am gets logged as a 9am departure and an 11am arrival. Future work should also consider modeling trips with a finer level of granularity.

These clock hours are then placed on an NHTS **travel day** (4am → 3:59am next day): hours 0–3 belong to the calendar morning after the travel-day start date, and overnight trips may span midnight.

Notes:
- If we assume that all households have at most one EV, then we should not be matching trip profiles based on the number of vehicles (this is fixed, now there is the match_on_vehicles flag)
- Right now code assumes if multiple vehicles, they are matched in the same 'tier'
- as a pre-processing step (or should this be done in load_nhts_data?), we recommend removing the bottom and top 10% (or 5%?) trip profiles in terms of daily miles traveled

## 4. Simulating hourly travel for a given period of time

Next, for a given simulation range, we generate hourly travel for each EV over the date range passed to the model. The simulation window **must** align with NHTS travel days: `start_date` at 04:00 and `end_date` at 03:00 (e.g. `2024-01-01T04:00:00` through `2025-01-01T03:00:00`). Date-only config values are rejected. From step 3, each EV has a matched weekday trip profile and a matched weekend trip profile drawn from NHTS. We do this as follows:

For each travel day in the simulation range (4am on date $D$ through 3:59am on $D+1$):
- Use the matched weekday profile if $D$ is Monday–Friday, and the matched weekend profile if it is Saturday or Sunday. If a vehicle is missing a profile for that day type, skip the travel day entirely (treat the vehicle as home).
- Map each NHTS clock hour onto the travel-day axis (hours before 4am → early morning on $D+1$).
- For each trip in that day's profile, independently draw a departure offset and an arrival offset from the discrete distribution on (-2, -1, 0, 1, 2) with probabilities p = (0.05, 0.10, 0.70, 0.10, 0.05), and draw miles traveled from a normal distribution centered at the logged miles with standard deviation equal to 10% of the mean.
- Add the sampled offsets in travel-day hours, then clip departures/arrivals to the travel-day window $[4, 28)$ (where 24–27 are clock hours 0–3 next day).
- Normalize the travel day's perturbed trips in departure order:
  - enforce at least one away hour per trip (`arrival > departure` on the travel-day axis)
  - if trips overlap, push later departures forward to the previous trip's arrival hour (the next trip may start immediately when the prior one ends)
  - cap arrivals at the travel-day end (4am next day), so trips cannot spill into the following travel day
  - drop any trip whose departure can no longer fit before the travel-day end, and drop all remaining trips after that point.
- Emit calendar `departure_date` / `arrival_date` with clock hours (overnight trips span midnight).
- Keep the perturbed miles for trips that survive normalization; drop miles for dropped trips.

Notes:
- Each NHTS trip carries a trip weight, which could maybe be used in the future. Right now, weights are used to permute the order the trips are sampled but have essentially no impact on the resulting trip draws (unless some trips are pushed past the travel-day end and dropped).

## 5. Three different EV charging schedules

From step 4, we can obtain:
- a vehicle presence schedule indicating which hours the EV is at home and available to charge and when it is away, and
- a discharge schedule with the number of kWh discharged per hour. Our model currently assumes that discharge for each trip happens evenly over the trip duration at a rate of 0.3 kWh per mile driven. In reality, this rate is impacted by temperature and this should be modeled in the future.

What remains is to model how the vehicle is charged. We assume that the EV's battery is lossless and has full charge at the start of the simulation range. We consider three charging models:
1. Immediate charging: As soon as the vehicle gets home, it immediately starts charging at maximum power until its battery is full or its next trip begins. If the battery does not have enough charge to fulfill a trip, we assume it uses public charging to obtain the remaining needed charge and it returns home at the originally scheduled arrival hour with an empty battery. These hours are flagged and it's likely that these trip profiles would not correspond to an EV owner (or that EV owner would have chosen a larger battery capacity). This should be considered with more detail in future work.

2. Cost minimizing charging: Assume perfect foresight over the vehicle's trips for the entire year and formulate an LP to schedule charging for the entire simulation range to minimize costs. To do this, we define the following. First, let $T$ be number of hours in the simulation range ($T=8,784$ for the 2024 leap-year travel-day window from 4am Jan 1 through 3am Jan 1 next year) and index it by $t=0, 1, \dots$ with $t=0$ corresponding to the first hour of the simulation window (4am on the start date when using the NHTS-aligned year).

Parameters:
- $p_t$: price of electricity in hour $t$ (in $/kWh)
- $x^{DB}_t$: power discharged from the EV at hour $t$ (in kW)
- $penalty_t^{SL}$: penalty for load shedding in hour $t$ (in $/kWh); set to $10,000 by default
- $C^B$: charging capacity (set to 7.2 kW for a Level 2 charger)
- $K^B$: battery capacity (set to 90.0 kWh by default)

Variables:
- $x^{CB}_t$: power charged to the EV at hour $t$ (in kW) $\forall t=0,\dots, T-1$
- $x^{SL}_t$: power of shed load at hour $t$ (in kW) $\forall t=0,\dots, T-1$
- $s_t$: EV battery's state of charge at hour $t$ (in kWh) $\forall t=0, \dots, T$; the initial state is set to $s_0 = 90.0$ kWh

The objective of the LP is to minimize 
$$
\sum_{t=0}^{T-1} p_t x^{CB}_t + penalty_t^{SL} x^{SL}_t
$$
subject to the constraints 
$$
s_1 = s_0 + x^{CB}_0 - x^{DB}_0 + x^{SL}_0, \\
s_{t+1} = s_t + x^{CB}_t - x^{DB}_t + x^{SL}_t \quad \forall t=1,\dots, T-1 \\
x_t^{CB} \leq C^B \quad \forall t=0,\dots, T-1 \\
s_t \leq K^B \quad \forall t=0, \dots, T \\
x^{CB}_t, x^{SL}_t, s_t \geq 0 \quad \forall t
$$

The solution to this LP corresponds to a lower bound on how much the EV owner pays for a given electricity rate. The shed load variables are included to guarantee feasibility, and it is likely that customers that would have to shed load (meaning it is not possible for their EV to meet their travel needs) either would not own an EV in the first place or need a vehicle with a larger battery. This requires some post-processing.

3. Off peak charging: 

This charging policy models how a customer might respond to a TOU rate (e.g., electricity is more expensive during peak hours from 5pm to 9pm). Our implementation is adapted from Sherry's work (https://github.com/switchbox-data/rate-design-platform-archive/blob/ba759a150a7763fa6a9e267c6c090dc7d135f0ca/docs/docs_tou_ev_schedule_value_learning.qmd), the only difference being that we allow charging during the day and she does not. 

Similar to the above LP, we assume perfect foresight of future trip schedules. For each day $d$ in the simulation range, we compute the minimum SOC needed for the day's trips with 
$$
SOC^{req}_d = \max (SOC^{\min}, \sum_{t \in d} x^{DB}_t + 0.2 * K^B),
$$
where we define 
- $SOC^{\min}$: household comfort threshold (e.g., 20% of battery capacity is currently assumed as default),
- $x^{DB}_t$: power discharged from the EV at hour $t$ (in kW), and
- $K^B$: battery capacity (set to 90.0 kWh by default).

At each hour $t =0,\dots,23$ on each day $d-1$, the battery is charged according to the policy:
$$
x^{CB}_t = \begin{cases}
\min(C^B, SOC^{req}_d - s_t, K^B - s_t) \text{ if } t \text{ is an off-peak hour, the vehicle is at home, and } s_t < SOC^{req}_d \\
0 \text{ otherwise}
\end{cases},
$$ where similar to policy 2, we define
- $x^{CB}_t$: power charged to the EV at hour $t$ (in kW) $\forall t=0,\dots, T-1$,
- $s_t$: EV battery's state of charge at hour $t$ (in kWh) $\forall t=0, \dots, T$; the initial state is set to $s_0 = 90.0$ kWh, and
- $C^B$: charging capacity (set to 7.2 kW for a Level 2 charger).

We use the same procedure as in policy 1 to deal with occassions where the battery does not have enough charge to complete a trip.

Notes:
- should we require SOC at the end to be the same as the start? (meaning we should have a terminal constraint that $s_T = s_0$)
- check implementation of policy 3 is correct (I think code currently only allows nighttime charging and not daytime!!!)
- future work should not assume perfect foresight (especially for policy 3)
